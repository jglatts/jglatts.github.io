Learning CNC Machining on a Haas Mill

By John Glatts

For most of my career, I have worked as a software engineer designing manufacturing automation, machine vision systems, custom electronics, test equipment, and production software.

Recently, I decided to expand that skill set by learning another side of the manufacturing process: CNC machining on a Haas mill.

At first, the control panel looked intimidating. There were work offsets, tool offsets, coordinate systems, unfamiliar buttons, and hundreds of lines of G-code.

After studying existing programs, asking questions, running dry cycles, and making my first chips, the machine began to feel much more approachable.

It was not magic.
It was another engineering system waiting to be understood.

Understanding G-Code

The first thing I wanted to understand was not simply which buttons to press. I wanted to understand what the machine was actually reading.

A CNC program is a sequence of commands that define positioning, motion, speed, tooling, offsets, and machine functions. 

G90

G00 X2.500 Y1.250
G01 Z-0.010 F5.0
G01 X4.000 Y1.250 F10.0

Reading G-code reminded me of reading low-level software. Each instruction performs one specific operation.

Individually, the commands are fairly simple. Together, they control an entire manufacturing process.

Once I stopped looking at the program as hundreds of unrelated lines and started seeing it as a structured sequence, it became much easier to follow.

Absolute and Incremental Positioning

One of the first important concepts was the difference between absolute and incremental positioning.

  • G90 uses absolute positioning
  • G91 uses incremental positioning

In absolute mode, the machine moves to a defined coordinate. In incremental mode, it moves a specified distance from its current position. 

G90

G00 X3.000 Y1.000

G91
G00 X0.500 Y-0.250

Coming from a software background, I thought of this as the difference between assigning a value and adding an offset to an existing value.

Once that comparison clicked, following machine movement became much easier.

Machine Coordinates and Work Coordinates

One of the biggest learning moments was understanding that the machine and the workpiece do not necessarily share the same coordinate system.

The Haas knows where its axes are located in machine coordinates. A work offset, such as G54, G58, or G59, tells the control where the part origin is located relative to the machine.

Write-Output coordinate_transform

Machine Coordinates → Work Offset → Part Coordinates

For example, our programs may use G58 with X and Y zero located at the lower-left corner of the stock and Z zero located at the top surface. 

G20 G17 G40 G49 G80 G90 G94

G58

Once the work offset is active, all programmed coordinates are interpreted relative to that part origin.

This concept felt familiar because coordinate transformations are also common in robotics, graphics, machine vision, and motion-control software.

Tool Length Offsets

Every cutting tool has a different physical length. Instead of rewriting every program for every cutter, the Haas stores the measured length of each tool in the tool offset table. 

T8 M06

G00 G43 H08 Z2.0000

In this example, T8 M06 loads Tool 8. The G43 H08 command applies the corresponding positive tool length compensation from offset register H08.

The program can then use meaningful Z coordinates based on the workpiece rather than the raw machine position.

Understanding the relationship between the tool length offset, work offset, and machine position removed much of the mystery surrounding Z-axis movement.

Touching Off the Workpiece

Correctly establishing the top of the workpiece is one of the most important parts of a CNC setup.

In our shop, we use a touch-off device with an indicator light. The tool is carefully jogged down until contact is detected, and the known height of the device is used to calculate the top surface of the stock.

This process connects three important values:

  • The machine-coordinate position
  • The active tool length offset
  • The work-coordinate Z offset

A mistake in any one of those values can cause a tool to cut too deeply, remain above the material, or move somewhere unexpected.

This is why the setup must be measured, recorded, and verified before running the program.

Watch for G92

Another important lesson was learning about G92.

A G92 offset can shift the active coordinate system in addition to the selected work offset. If one is left active unexpectedly, the displayed work coordinates may not represent what the operator thinks they represent. 

G92 Z-0.010

An unexpected G92 value can change cutting depth or positioning even when the primary work offset appears correct.

Before running an unfamiliar program, it is important to check whether a G92 offset is active and understand why it is being used.

Safety and Verification

One lesson experienced machinists emphasize repeatedly is simple: slow down and verify the setup.

Before pressing Cycle Start, I now try to confirm the following:

  • The correct program is loaded
  • The correct work offset is active
  • The stock is located and clamped correctly
  • The correct tool is installed
  • The correct H offset is called
  • The tool length value is reasonable
  • The Z zero location matches the program comments
  • No unexpected G92 offset is active
  • Rapid moves have adequate clearance
  • Spindle speeds and feed rates are appropriate

A good CNC program should also begin with a safe startup line that cancels any unwanted modal states left behind by a previous program. 

G20 G17 G40 G49 G80 G90 G94

This establishes inch mode, the XY plane, absolute positioning, feed-per-minute mode, and cancels several forms of compensation and canned cycles.

Graphics, Dry Run, and Single Block

The Haas control provides several tools that make it possible to verify a program before committing to a full-speed cut.

  • Graphics Mode previews the programmed toolpath
  • Dry Run allows movement to be observed at a controlled rate
  • Single Block pauses after each block of code
  • Feed Hold stops controlled axis motion

These tools are especially useful when running a new program, using a new setup, or reviewing code generated by CAM software.

A few minutes spent verifying the first move, first plunge, and first tool change can prevent a very expensive mistake.

Making the First Chips

The first successful cut was incredibly satisfying.

Watching the spindle follow a toolpath that I understood, seeing the cutter remove material, and measuring the resulting feature made the entire process feel real.

Every successful setup builds confidence. Every operation adds another piece to the mental model.

Even simple exercises such as facing a blank, engraving initials, or cutting a basic contour provide valuable experience with coordinates, feeds, speeds, tooling, and machine control.

CAD, CAM, and the Post Processor

Learning CNC machining has also helped connect the full workflow between design and production.

A typical machining workflow may look like this:

CAM software can generate thousands of lines of code, but the operator still needs to understand the setup, coordinate system, tooling, and machine behavior.

The post processor translates the CAM toolpath into the specific G-code dialect and formatting expected by the Haas control.

Even when the code is generated automatically, being able to read it remains an important troubleshooting and safety skill.

Machining Makes Better Engineers

Learning CNC machining has already changed the way I think about mechanical design and manufacturing automation.

A CAD model can look perfect on a screen while still being difficult to fixture, measure, reach with a cutting tool, or produce economically.

Spending time at the machine creates a greater appreciation for:

  • Workholding
  • Tool access
  • Setup time
  • Tolerance selection
  • Material removal
  • Tool deflection
  • Surface finish
  • Inspection requirements
  • Operator safety

Likewise, a software background is helpful when analyzing G-code, visualizing coordinate transformations, troubleshooting machine logic, and automating repetitive manufacturing processes.

The disciplines reinforce one another.

Learning from Experience

No engineer develops entirely on their own.

Books, manuals, videos, and software are valuable, but there is no substitute for learning directly from people with years of practical experience.

Experienced machinists do much more than operate equipment. They understand materials, tooling, machine behavior, setup strategy, inspection, and the small details that determine whether a job succeeds.

Being able to ask questions and understand not only what someone is doing, but why they are doing it, has made the learning process far more valuable.

Final Thoughts

Every engineer starts somewhere.

The first software application, the first circuit board, the first CAD model, and the first CNC program can all feel intimidating.

With curiosity, practice, patience, and guidance from experienced people, those intimidating systems gradually become useful tools.

Learning the Haas has reminded me that engineering is a lifelong process. There is always another discipline to explore, another machine to understand, and another problem to solve.

That is one of the things I enjoy most about engineering.
