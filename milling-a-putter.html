Designing and Milling My First Putter

By John Glatts

For most of my engineering career, the things I design eventually become physical parts.

Sometimes they are 3D printed components. Sometimes they are circuit boards, test fixtures, automation systems, tooling, or production equipment.

Recently, after spending more time learning CNC machining, I wanted to try something different.

I wanted to design and machine a golf putter.

Not because I needed another putter.

I wanted to see if I could take an idea from a blank CAD screen all the way to a finished piece of aluminum that I could actually putt with.

Starting with a Blank CAD Model

I decided to start with a mallet-style putter.

There was no detailed manufacturing drawing to follow and no existing process. The first step was simply figuring out what I wanted the putter to look like.

I modeled the first version in Fusion 360.

That meant working through the basic geometry of the head, including the overall length and width, face height, sole geometry, rear profile, shaft location, radii, fillets, and weight distribution.

It also meant thinking about something that becomes increasingly important once you start machining your own designs:

Can this actually be manufactured?

There is a major difference between designing something that looks good in CAD and designing something that can realistically be machined.

The CNC machine does not care how good the render looks.

The cutter still needs to reach the feature.
The stock still needs to be held.
And somehow, after machining the first side, the part needs to be held again.

Printing Version 1

Before putting a block of aluminum on the Haas, I 3D printed the first version of the putter.

This was a quick way to turn the CAD model into something physical before committing to the machining process.

Holding the printed part immediately showed things that were difficult to judge on a computer screen.

The overall size felt different. The proportions were easier to evaluate. The rear shape and face dimensions were much easier to understand once the design was sitting in my hands.

Even though the printed version was never intended to become the finished putter, it was an extremely useful prototype.

That is one of the reasons I continue to like combining additive and subtractive manufacturing.

A 3D printer can turn a design into a physical object very quickly.

CNC machining forces you to think about how that object will actually be manufactured.

Moving into Fusion 360 CAM

Once I was comfortable with the basic design, I started creating the machining operations in Fusion 360.

This project also became one of my first larger attempts at using Fusion for CAM.

The workflow quickly started making sense to me because the CAD model, stock, tooling, coordinate system, and machining operations all exist inside the same environment.

For the first operation, the goal was to remove most of the material surrounding the putter while leaving enough stock to securely hold the part.

A 3D adaptive clearing operation handled much of the bulk material removal.

Watching the simulated toolpath was interesting because the software was solving a machining problem that would be extremely tedious to program manually.

Instead of writing thousands of individual machine movements, I could define the model, stock, cutter, feeds, speeds, stepdowns, and machining strategy.

Fusion generated the toolpath.

The post processor then translated that toolpath into G-code that the Haas could execute.

Write-Output manufacturing_pipeline

Fusion CAD → Fusion CAM → Post Processor → Haas G-Code

Coming from software engineering, this workflow feels surprisingly natural.

The CAD model defines the object.

CAM defines the manufacturing strategy.

The post processor acts almost like a compiler.

And the CNC machine executes the resulting instructions.

From Simulation to Aluminum

Eventually there is a point where the simulation has to become real.

For the first machined version, I started with a block of 6061 aluminum.

The stock was secured to the machine, the work coordinate system was established, the tools were checked, and the program was reviewed before cutting.

Then I pressed Cycle Start.

Watching the first tool begin removing material was one of the most satisfying parts of the project.

A rectangular block of aluminum slowly started becoming something recognizable.

First there was a rough outline.
Then the major features became visible.
Eventually it started looking like a putter.

There is something different about machining a design that you created yourself.

Every movement of the cutter represents a decision made earlier in CAD or CAM.

When the machine follows the expected path, it confirms that the entire chain is working: design, setup, tooling, coordinates, CAM, post processing, and machine control.

Workholding Became Part of the Design

One of the biggest lessons from the project had very little to do with the actual shape of the putter.

It was workholding.

During the first operation, holding a rectangular piece of aluminum was relatively straightforward.

After most of that material was removed, the problem changed.

Now I had a partially machined putter surrounded by the remaining stock.

I still needed access to additional surfaces, but I also needed a reliable way to hold the part without damaging finished geometry.

That forced me to think about the second operation before the first operation was finished.

  • Where can the clamps go?
  • What material needs to remain after Op 1?
  • Where can tabs be placed?
  • What surfaces will exist after the first operation?
  • How will the part be located for Op 2?
  • Where will the new work coordinate system be established?

Those questions changed the way I looked at the CAD model.

The machining setup was no longer something that happened after the design.

It became part of the design.

Tool Clearance Is Real

The project also provided a very practical lesson in tool clearance.

During one version of the setup, the cutter contacted a screw being used for workholding.

Fortunately, the situation was stopped, but it was a good reminder of how many physical objects exist inside a real CNC machine that may not exist inside the CAM simulation.

The software understands the model, stock, tools, and fixtures that I define.

It does not automatically understand every screw, clamp, parallel, or piece of hardware that I decide to place inside the machine.

Clamps occupy real space.
Screws occupy real space.
The cutter occupies real space.

That sounds obvious.

It becomes considerably more memorable when carbide and steel try to occupy the same space.

The next revision changed the machining strategy and the screw locations to provide significantly more clearance.

Every mistake adds another item to the mental checklist.

Why 6061 Aluminum?

For the first machined version, I chose 6061 aluminum.

It is readily available, machines well, and is a good material for learning.

More importantly, it allowed me to focus on the manufacturing process rather than making the first putter unnecessarily complicated.

The goal of Version 1 was not to build the world's most advanced putter.

The goal was to complete the entire process.

CAM Changed How I Look at CAD

This project reinforced something I have been learning since spending more time machining.

Manufacturing experience changes the way you design parts.

Before learning machining, it is easy to create a feature simply because the CAD software allows it.

After standing in front of the machine, different questions start appearing automatically.

  • Can an end mill reach this feature?
  • What diameter cutter will fit here?
  • How deep is the pocket compared with the tool diameter?
  • How am I going to hold the part?
  • Can this be machined in one setup?
  • What happens when the part is flipped?
  • Where am I going to establish the work offset?
  • Will the toolholder or cutter hit a clamp?

Suddenly the CAD model is not just geometry.

It is a manufacturing plan.

Software Engineering Meets Machining

One of my favorite parts of learning CNC machining has been discovering how much of my software background transfers into it.

CAM software is incredibly complex.

It takes geometry, machining constraints, tool definitions, feeds, speeds, linking moves, stock conditions, and machine configuration and turns them into thousands of coordinated movements.

Eventually, those operations become instructions similar to: 

G90

G54

T6 M06
G00 G43 H06 Z1.0000

G00 X2.500 Y1.250
G01 Z-0.050 F5.0
G01 X4.000 Y1.250 F12.0

The Haas then executes those instructions.

That is both the power and the danger of CNC machining.

The machine is going to follow the programmed instructions.

The engineering needs to happen before Cycle Start.

Understanding that has made me appreciate both the software running behind modern manufacturing and the machinists who understand how to use it safely and effectively.

Version 1 Is Supposed to Be Version 1

It is tempting when designing something personal to keep changing the CAD model until everything is perfect.

But eventually you need to make the part.

Version 1 does not need to be perfect.

It needs to exist.

Once the first putter is machined, I can evaluate things that are much harder to understand from a computer screen.

  • Overall head size
  • Weight
  • Balance
  • Appearance at address
  • Surface finish
  • Machining accessibility
  • Workholding strategy
  • Features that should change on Version 2

Those lessons can go directly into the next design.

That is engineering.

More Than a Putter

At the end of the day, this project is not really about needing a new golf club.

I already have a putter.

The interesting part is being able to make one.

A few years ago, I could design software that controlled manufacturing equipment.

Then I started designing more mechanical components and production tooling.

Then I started learning the Haas.

Now I can sit down at a computer, design a putter, generate the machining strategy, walk into the shop, load a block of aluminum, and start turning that design into a physical part.

That complete process is what makes the project exciting to me.

Engineering Is Better When You Make Things

One of the biggest things CNC machining has reinforced for me is how valuable it is for engineers to make things.

CAD teaches geometry.

CAM teaches manufacturing strategy.

Machining teaches reality.

The cutter deflects.
The stock moves if it is not held correctly.
Tools have physical limits.
Workholding takes space.
Surface finish matters.
Measurements matter.

And sometimes the part of the project that looked easy in CAD becomes the hardest part of the entire job.

Those lessons are difficult to fully appreciate from behind a computer screen.

What's Next?

The first mallet is only the beginning.

There are already things I want to change and other designs I want to try.

One of those is a more traditional blade-style putter.

Each version creates another opportunity to improve the CAD, CAM, workholding, machining strategy, and ultimately the finished club.

That is probably what I enjoy most about this project.

There is always another iteration.

Another setup to figure out.
Another toolpath to improve.
Another piece of aluminum waiting to become something.

Final Thoughts

Designing and milling my first putter combined several things I enjoy: software, CAD, CAM, manufacturing, machining, and golf.

More importantly, it gave me another opportunity to take something that originally existed only on a computer screen and make it real.

The first version will not be the last.

There will be design changes, better setups, better toolpaths, and probably completely different putter styles.

But the first one is important because it proves the entire process works.

Start with an idea.
Model it.
Figure out how to make it.
And make some chips.

That is one of the things I enjoy most about engineering.
